# Reading list: papers that can still move this pipeline

Organised by pipeline stage. For each paper: what it shows, and the concrete experiment it suggests for
this code base (see `docs/REPORT.md` for the current numbers the experiments would be judged against).

## 1. Candidate generation (blocking) - the recall ceiling

| paper | why it matters here | experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Papadakis et al., *A Survey of Blocking and Filtering Techniques for Entity Resolution*, ACM CSUR 2020 ([arXiv 1905.06167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06167)) | Taxonomy of blocking, meta-blocking and filtering; shows that combining several cheap key families and pruning the candidate graph beats any single key. | Our blocking already unions three TF-IDF blocks; the survey's *meta-blocking* (prune edges by graph weight, keep per-node top-k) is the principled version of our `--topk` cut. |
| Thirumuruganathan et al., *Deep Learning for Blocking in Entity Matching: A Design Space Exploration*, PVLDB 2021 ([paper](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol14/p2459-thirumuruganathan.pdf)) | Systematic comparison of learned (embedding) blockers vs. TF-IDF; learned blockers win mostly on textual attributes, TF-IDF stays competitive on short structured strings. | Measure recall@k of a small sentence-embedding blocker (MiniLM-class, Apache-2.0, far below 8B) on the name string only, as a *fourth* block, and check whether it recovers the 1.2 % retrieval misses. |
| Wang & Zhang, *Pre-trained Language Models for Entity Blocking: A Reproducibility Study*, NAACL 2024; Brinkmann et al., *SC-Block: Supervised Contrastive Blocking*, ESWC 2024 ([arXiv 2303.03132](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.03132)) | Supervised contrastive blockers trained on the *matcher's own training pairs* give higher recall at the same k than unsupervised embeddings. | We have 7.6 M labelled pairs; a contrastive name encoder trained on them is cheap and legal (Apache-2.0 models). |
| *Towards Universal Dense Blocking for Entity Resolution* ([arXiv 2404.14831](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.14831)); *Neural Locality Sensitive Hashing for Entity Blocking* ([arXiv 2401.18064](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.18064)) | Dense blocking that transfers across domains without retraining; LSH to keep it linear-time. | Relevant for France (unseen country): a dense name blocker does not depend on hand-written French tables. |
| ING WBAA / D&B posts on `sparse_dot_topn`: TF-IDF character n-grams + sparse top-n matrix product for company names ([post](https://bergvca.github.io/2017/10/14/super-fast-string-matching.html), [D&B](https://medium.com/dnb-data-science-hub/company-name-matching-6a6330710334)) | Same mechanism as our `blocking.py`, but on character 3-grams rather than word tokens; character n-grams are robust to the typos (`Co1lege`, `Rmefinery`) that currently cause retrieval misses. | Add a `g:` block of character trigrams of the compact name. |

## 2. Matching model and features

| paper | why it matters here | experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Konda et al., *Magellan: Toward Building Entity Matching Management Systems*, PVLDB 2016 ([paper](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol9/p1197-pkonda.pdf)) | The reference design for feature-based ER with tree ensembles: automatic feature generation per attribute type, blocking debugging, and the argument that random forests on similarity features are hard to beat on structured records. | Our feature set is close to Magellan's; its *feature-per-attribute-type* generator is a checklist for missing similarities (e.g. Monge-Elkan, affine gap on names). |
| Li et al., *Deep Entity Matching with Pre-Trained Language Models* (Ditto), PVLDB 2021 ([arXiv 2004.00584](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00584)); Brunner & Stockinger, *Effective entity matching with transformers*, VLDBJ 2023 ([link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-023-00779-z)) | Fine-tuned small transformers (DistilBERT-class, Apache-2.0) as pair classifiers, with domain-knowledge "span highlighting" and augmentation (token shuffling, deletion), which are exactly the corruptions in this data. | A DistilBERT pair classifier as a *third-stage* feature on the 3 candidates per record (cheap at inference) is the single biggest untried modelling change; Ditto reports 96.5 % F1 on a 789k x 412k company dataset. |
| Peeters, Steiner & Bizer, *Entity Matching using Large Language Models*, EDBT 2025 ([arXiv 2310.11244](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11244v4)); *Fine-tuning Large Language Models for Entity Matching* ([arXiv 2409.08185](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.08185)) | Where LLM matchers help (unseen entities, corner cases) and what they cost; fine-tuned small models close most of the gap. | Not for the full 30 M pairs, but useful as a labeller for an error-analysis sample or for the ambiguous `p in [0.3, 0.7]` band. |
| Gillick et al., *Learning Dense Representations for Entity Retrieval* ([arXiv 1909.10506](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.10506)); *Block-SCL* ([arXiv 2207.02008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.02008)) | Hard-negative mining: train on the negatives the blocker actually produces, not random ones (+2.8 points reported). | We already train on blocking negatives; the paper's *iterative* mining (re-block with the current model, add newly confusable pairs) is the next step, and directly targets the namesake errors in section 8 of the report. |
| Primpeli & Bizer, *Profiling Entity Matching Benchmark Tasks*, CIKM 2020 ([pdf](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/media/Einrichtungen/dws/Files_Research/Web-based_Systems/pub/CIKM2020_Primpeli_Bizer.pdf)); Peeters et al., *WDC Products: A Multi-Dimensional Entity Matching Benchmark*, EDBT 2024 ([arXiv 2301.09521](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09521)) | Defines *corner cases* and *unseen-entity* generalisation as the two dimensions that decide ER difficulty, and shows that random splits hide both. | This is the justification for our name-group folds and leave-one-country-out; their corner-case profiler can quantify how hard our train vs. test splits are. |

## 3. Decision rule: thresholds, calibration, prior shift, one-to-one assignment

| paper | why it matters here | experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Lipton, Elkan & Narayanaswamy, *Optimal Thresholding of Classifiers to Maximize F1 Measure*, ECML-PKDD 2014 ([arXiv 1402.1892](https://arxiv.org/abs/1402.1892)) | For calibrated probabilities the F1-optimal threshold equals half the optimal F1; the paper covers F-beta, so the same holds for F0.5 with beta-dependent constants. | Cross-check: our tuned threshold 0.79 on calibrated OOF probabilities vs. the closed-form prediction. If they agree, the threshold can be set analytically per country from the calibrated scores alone, which matters for France where no OOF estimate exists. |
| Saerens, Latinne & Decaestecker, *Adjusting the Outputs of a Classifier to New a Priori Probabilities: A Simple Procedure*, Neural Computation 2002 ([link](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1162/089976602753284446)); Alexandari, Kundaje & Shrikumar, *Maximum Likelihood with Bias-Corrected Calibration is Hard-To-Beat at Label Shift Adaptation*, ICML 2020 ([arXiv 1901.06852](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.06852)); Sipka et al., *The Hitchhiker's Guide to Prior-Shift Adaptation*, WACV 2022 ([pdf](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2022/papers/Sipka_The_Hitchhikers_Guide_to_Prior-Shift_Adaptation_WACV_2022_paper.pdf)) | EM estimation of the *test* class prior from the unlabelled test predictions, then Bayes correction. Our `select_threshold.py` estimates the shift from record counts; EM estimates it from the model's own test scores. | Run Saerens EM on the calibrated test probabilities to estimate the test positive rate; compare with the record-count estimate (r = 2.02). Two independent estimates that agree are a strong check before submitting. |
| Guo et al., *On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks*, ICML 2017 ([arXiv 1706.04599](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)) | Temperature scaling (one parameter) vs. Platt (two) vs. isotonic; the one-parameter version is the robust default. | Our Platt fit has a ~ 0.8; temperature-only is the ablation to run if Platt ever misbehaves again under shift. |
| Papadakis et al., *An analysis of one-to-one matching algorithms for entity resolution*, VLDBJ 2023 ([link](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1007/s00778-023-00791-3)); *Bipartite Graph Matching Algorithms for Clean-Clean ER* ([arXiv 2112.14030](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.14030)) | Empirical comparison of eight ways to turn a bipartite similarity graph into one-to-one links (Hungarian, unique mapping, row-column, ...). | Our assignment is "best Source 1 per record" (a one-sided greedy rule). The paper's *unique-mapping clustering* and *row-column assignment* are cheap alternatives that would resolve the namesake ties in the error analysis. |
| Bhattacharya & Getoor, *Collective Entity Resolution in Relational Data*, TKDD 2007 ([pdf](https://linqs.org/assets/resources/bhattacharya-tkdd07.pdf)) | Decisions about one pair should inform decisions about related pairs. Our stage 2 is a one-step version of this. | Iterating stage 2 (feed stage-2 probabilities back as context) is a two-line experiment. |

## 4. Text normalisation, transliteration, addresses

| paper | why it matters here | experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Madhani et al., *Aksharantar: Open Indic-language Transliteration datasets and models for the Next Billion Users*, EMNLP Findings 2023 ([arXiv 2205.03018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.03018)); IndicXlit models (MIT, ~11 M parameters, [GitHub](https://github.com/AI4Bharat/IndicXlit)) | A trained Indic-to-Roman transliterator for 21 scripts. Our lexicon covers frequent tokens only; retrieval misses concentrate on Indic records (section 8 of the report). | Replace the unidecode fallback with IndicXlit for lexicon misses (check the competition's "no external data" rule: the model is a pretrained MIT model, the dataset stays untouched). |
| Steinberger & Pouliquen, *Multilingual person name recognition and transliteration* ([arXiv cs/0609051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0609051)) | Name-variant matching across scripts via normalisation + phonetic keys rather than translation. | Our `phonetic.py` skeleton is unused in blocking; add it as a block for Indic-script records. |
| Deepparse: *An Extendable and Fine-Tunable State-of-the-Art Library for Multinational Address Parsing* ([arXiv 2311.11846](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.11846)); libpostal ([design notes](https://www.mapzen.com/blog/inside-libpostal/)) | Learned address parsers (house number / road / city / state) instead of our regex component split; libpostal's digit-to-`D` normalisation trick for numbers. | Parse addresses into fields once, then compare field-by-field; the house-number features are the most valuable group in our ablation, so a better house-number extractor pays directly. |
| *Transformer-based Entity Legal Form Classification* ([arXiv 2310.12766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12766)) | Legal-form detection as a learned task instead of a suffix list; relevant for France (SARL/SAS/EURL variants). | Validate our `STRICT_LEGAL` / `WEAK_LEGAL` tables against their label set. |

## 5. Validation and leakage

| paper | why it matters here | experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Kapoor & Narayanan, *Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science*, Patterns 2023 ([arXiv 2207.07048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07048)) | Taxonomy of 8 leakage types; "preprocessing on train+test" and "non-independent train/test" are exactly the two we found. Proposes *model info sheets*. | Fill in a model info sheet for the submission; it is a one-page document and the leakage audit in `docs/REPORT.md` already answers most items. |
| Fellegi & Sunter (1969) via *Revisiting the probabilistic method of record linkage* ([arXiv 1911.01874](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.01874)) and Splink ([GitHub](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink)) | The classical unsupervised model estimates match/non-match likelihoods per comparison with EM, no labels needed. | An unsupervised Fellegi-Sunter score on the *test* split is a label-free sanity check of our supervised probabilities under shift, and a candidate feature that adapts to the test distribution by construction. |

## Suggested order

1. Character-trigram block + phonetic block for Indic records (blocking recall; cheapest, measured by `blocking_recall.json`).
2. Saerens EM estimate of the test prior vs. the record-count ratio (a check, not a change).
3. One-to-one assignment variants from Papadakis et al. 2023 on the OOF predictions (`tools/robustness.py` scaffolding fits).
4. IndicXlit in place of unidecode, if the rules allow a pretrained transliterator.
5. DistilBERT pair classifier on the top-3 candidates as a stage-3 feature (largest expected gain, largest cost).
